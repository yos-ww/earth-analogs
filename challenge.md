# Hackathon Challenge: Earth Analogs for Lunar and Martian Exploration

## Summary

Selecting future landing sites and lunar base locations depends on many factors, including topography, geology, environment, and available resources. Places on Earth with characteristics analogous to the Moon or Mars (the Atacama Desert, Haughton Crater, Death Valley) let mission teams test equipment and methods before they fly. However, such terrestrial analog sites are not entirely well characterized.

**Your challenge:** use open Earth, Moon, and Mars data from NASA and other space agencies to identify and characterize new terrestrial analogs for future bases or landing sites. Draw on deserts, polar regions, volcanic and arid terrains, caves, and other environments that share conditions with the lunar or Martian surface. Then present your findings as a map, visualization, or other tool.

## Background

Earth is our most accessible laboratory for preparing to explore the Moon and Mars. Scientists and engineers already use terrestrial "analog" environments (deserts, polar regions, volcanic landscapes, impact craters, and caves) to study planetary processes and test instruments, technologies, and exploration concepts. No location on Earth perfectly reproduces another planetary environment, and different locations may be useful analogs for different characteristics.

As humanity prepares for sustained exploration of the Moon and future human exploration of Mars, open Earth, Moon, and Mars data could be used to search globally and systematically for terrestrial environments that may be useful, and perhaps previously unrecognized, analogs. Possible dimensions:

- Geology and surface materials
- Terrain type and topography
- Atmospheric and aeolian processes such as dust storms
- Temperature and other environmental extremes
- Water or ice
- Biological and habitability characteristics
- Subsurface environments
- Conditions relevant to robotic or human operations

The goal is **not** simply to rediscover well-known analog sites, or to find one place that simply looks like the Moon or Mars. Participants are encouraged to:

- Combine datasets creatively to identify and characterize locations anywhere on Earth.
- Explain what makes them analogous and what does not.
- Show how these environments could help conduct lunar or Mars science and exploration.

Solutions might range from a compelling individual site, environment, or geophysical process to maps, similarity metrics, machine learning approaches, or other innovative methods for discovering and comparing planetary analogs. Data may come from NASA and other space agency missions (Earth, Moon, Mars), as well as data and images obtained locally.

## Objectives

Search our own planet for places, environments, and processes that could serve as analogs and help us explore the Moon and Mars.

Using open NASA Earth data together with available Moon and Mars data from other space agencies and open sources, identify and characterize one or more locations on Earth that could serve as useful analogs for:

- Candidate permanent Moon-base locations, or
- Future Mars landing and exploration sites.

Explore what makes your Earth location analogous. You might compare data and images of:

- Geology and surface materials
- Terrain and topography
- Temperature and environmental extremes
- Water or ice
- Dust and atmospheric processes
- Biology and habitability
- Caves or subsurface environments
- Conditions relevant to robotic and human exploration

Your analog does not need to match the Moon or Mars in every way. Explain:

1. Which characteristics or processes provide a strong analogy.
2. Where the analogy breaks down.
3. How studying or using your Earth analog could help prepare for exploration of the Moon or Mars.

### Format is open

Be creative in how you search for, analyze, and communicate your analog. Options include:

- Written report
- Interactive map
- Visualization
- App
- Story
- Similarity-ranking system
- ML tool
- Another innovative approach

You might investigate a single location, compare several locations around the world, or develop a method for systematically searching Earth for previously unrecognized analogs. Consider also documenting your findings in written form to share with other enthusiasts and to educate the public.

## Potential Considerations

You may (but are not required to) consider the following.

1. **Think beyond visual similarity.** Two places may look alike but differ substantially in geology, environment, or physical processes. Use data to explain why your proposed location is a meaningful analog.

2. **Consider different dimensions of analogy:**
   - Geology, mineralogy, and surface materials
   - Terrain, elevation, slope, roughness, craters, and other topographic features
   - Temperature, illumination, and environmental extremes
   - Water, ice, permafrost, and other potential resources
   - Dust, wind, erosion, and other atmospheric or surface processes
   - Biology, extremophiles, habitability, and biosignatures
   - Caves, lava tubes, underground environments, and other subsurface features
   - Conditions relevant to landing, mobility, navigation, robotic operations, human exploration, or construction

3. **An analog does not have to match everything.** A location might be an excellent geological analog but a poor temperature analog. Identify what is similar, how similar, and what is different.

4. **Think about processes as well as places.** Sedimentation, dust storms, volcanic activity, erosion, ice dynamics, or water-driven alteration may provide insight into processes occurring today, or that occurred in the past, on the Moon or Mars.

5. **Look beyond well-known analog sites.** Antarctica, the Atacama Desert, Iceland, and Hawaii are already widely studied. NASA's global Earth-observation datasets may reveal compelling analogs in places that have received much less attention.

6. **Explore the whole Earth, including below its surface.** Polar regions, deserts, mountains, volcanoes, impact craters, salt flats, glaciers, caves, lava tubes, mines, and other environments are all fair game.

7. **Use multiple datasets when useful.** Combining observations of terrain, temperature, geology, vegetation, water, ice, aerosols, or other characteristics may reveal relationships that are hard to see in a single dataset.

8. **Consider scale.** A continent or desert need not be treated as a single analog. A particular crater, valley, volcanic field, cave system, or even a small area within a larger region could be a much stronger match.

9. **Connect your analog to exploration.** Explain how the location or process could help scientists and engineers to:
   - Understand the Moon, Mars, or both
   - Test instruments or technologies
   - Prepare robotic or human operations
   - Search for resources or signs of habitability
   - Reduce exploration risks

10. **Be creative with your approach.** Maps and visualizations are welcome, but solutions could also use GIS, statistical analysis, similarity metrics, machine learning, interactive applications, games, or other innovative methods.

11. **Remember Earth's limits as an analog.** Earth cannot reproduce reduced gravity, the lunar vacuum, the thin Martian atmosphere, or the radiation environments of the Moon and Mars. Recognizing these differences is part of good scientific analysis.

12. **Surprise us.** A strong solution may identify an unexpected Earth location, reveal an unexpected connection between Earth and the Moon or Mars, or introduce a new way of using Earth data to help enable future exploration.
